First, your bearings

AI is a bigger pie. Today is one slice.

What it actually does

AI gives you the convincing average.

It predicts the most plausible next words. Fluent, well-shaped, usually about right.
Your alarm

AI's mistakes don't look like mistakes.

You spot weak work by its signals. AI's wrong answers don't carry them.
The signals you rely on
A person's weak draft
AI's wrong answer
Typos and slips
Gaps, TODOs, missing pieces
Hedging: "I think", "probably"
Uneven formatting, a rushed ending
Your alarm
Rings
Stays quiet
Both are wrong. Only one of them looks it.
The second trap

Sharp where you know. Trusting where you don't.

In your own field
You catch its errors straight away.
"That's not how our contracts work." "That figure's from last year." You know enough to see it.
Outside it
The market, the law, the numbers.
Same tool, same error rate. But it reads as fact, because you can't see the mistakes.
The defence: be most sceptical where you know least. "Gell-Mann amnesia": Michael Crichton, 2002, after the physicist Murray Gell-Mann

Tool 1 · The trust tool

Two questions before you act on it.

Question 1 · Precision
How exact must it be?
Average
The ballpark is fine. A draft, a summary, ideas to react to.
Precise
One right answer. A figure, a name, a date, a citation, a clause.
Question 2 · Consequences
What happens if it's wrong?
Small
Cheap to fix, easy to reverse, nobody harmed.
Large
Money, safety, reputation, someone's job or wellbeing.
Two questions, ten seconds, four corners.

Before the break

Three rules before you paste.

01
Classify it, then pick the tool.
Public, internal, confidential or regulated? Anything internal or above goes only into tools your organisation has approved for it.
02
Leave out what identifies people.
Names, health details, client data, anything under NDA. Describe the shape of the data instead of pasting it.
03
Know what it remembers.
Saved memories, inferred memories and chat history are kept separately. Deleting a chat may not delete what it learned. Use a temporary chat for anything sensitive.
Check yours now, two minutes. Open your tool's data settings. Is it training on what you type? Is memory on?
And: your name goes on the output, not the tool's.
Session 2 · Brief

Brief it like a capable new colleague.

No magic words. Smart, fast, and brand new to your job.
What you know
  • Who it's for, and what they care about
  • What happened last time
  • The politics
  • What good looks like here
What it knows
  • How emails, reports and plans usually go
  • A great deal of public information
  • Nothing about your client, your team or your standards
Tool 2 · RTCF

Four parts of a good brief.

R
Role
Who should it write as?
A senior HR adviser in a mid-sized Australian firm
T
Task
What exactly, and how much?
Draft a reply. Under 200 words.
C
Context
What does it need to know?
18-month client. Their board shifted priorities. I want a meeting, not a rewrite.
Does most of the work
F
Format
What should it look like?
Email. Acknowledge, explain, propose, close.
Not every prompt needs all four. Anything that matters does.
Tool 2 · RTCF

One minute of briefing. Thirty minutes saved.

Before
Write an email about the project delay.
Generic, polite, and unusable without a rewrite.
After

TASK Write to the client about a two-week delivery delay.
CONTEXT A vendor's API integration ran late. The client values transparency and the relationship is good. New date: 15 March.
FORMAT Under 200 words. Acknowledge, explain, new date, recommit.
Close to something you'd send.

Tool 2 · RTCF upgrades

Weak models can't infer. So leave nothing to infer.

Four upgrades. They help smaller, older and free-tier models most, and the best models still do better with them.
+ Example
Show it one you liked.
Match the length and tone of this example: [paste]
+ Done
Say what done looks like.
Done means under 200 words, figures only from my notes, and anything you're unsure of marked [CHECK].
+ Questions
Let it ask first.
Before you start, ask me up to five questions.
+ Steps
Split the job.
Give me an outline only. We'll draft one section at a time.
Agreeable is not honest

Push back. Three ways.

Models are tuned to please. Disagree with no evidence and many will fold.
01 · Set a guardrail
Keep it honest.
Only change your assessment if I give you a new fact. Name the fact that changed it.
02 · Devil's advocate
Make it attack.
Don't flatter me. Give me three reasons this fails, and name the assumption it all rests on.
03 · Board of directors
Hear a room, not a voice.
Respond as a board: a CFO, a sceptical customer and a frontline manager. One paragraph each, then where they disagree.
You decide what survives. And for an independent read, start a fresh chat: long chats drift towards your view.

Tool 3 · The check loop

Five ways a smooth answer goes wrong.

"Are you sure?" doesn't count. The check comes from outside the tool.
01
Miscounts
Especially after filtering or grouping.
02
Correlation sold as cause
"Sunday tickets are slower, so Sundays cause complaints."
03
Silent gaps
Skips missing or messy rows without saying so.
04
Numbers that can't all be true
Shares that add to 112%. An average above the maximum.
05
Invented detail
A data point, quote or source that isn't in the file.
The fix · VET it
Verify. Explain. Test.
Verify the source yourself. Explain it back in your own words. Test the edges: when would it be wrong?
Check for skew too

Bias hasn't gone. It has gone quiet.

Models are trained hard against the obvious stereotypes. The skew lives in the small choices.
Try it · 3 minutes
Write a one-paragraph scene of a respected senior manager on a busy Monday.
New chat: the same, for a respected administrative officer.
Read them side by side
What is each one called? Who is warm, who is capable? Who holds authority in the scene?
That picture does quiet work every time you draft a position description, a reference or a performance review.
The generic output inherits the generic average. Noticing the skew is your job.

Decide like me · SIM

Teach it your rules. Keep the call.

One question picks the method: could you tell a new starter, out loud, why you decide the way you do?
S · Say
Yes? Talk it through.
Most people trim the exceptions when they type. Out loud, they stay in. Then let it ask you up to five follow-up questions.
I · Interview
"It depends"? Let it ask.
It questions you in turn until every "it depends" is an if-then rule. Each branch is a call you've been making without writing it down.
M · Mine
On file somewhere? Mine it.
Past requests and what you decided. It works out the rules you were following, including the ones you never wrote down.
All three end the same way: a page of if-then rules. Read it like a brief. Would these rules make the calls you make?
Save · test · fix

Don't take its word. Take the test.

Save it
Same chat: "Write these rules up so I can reuse them."
Rules, not stories: take out the names, clients and figures. Save it where your tool keeps standing instructions. Agent tools call a saved brief like this a skill. More on that at the end of the day.
Then the blind test
Three past cases. Inputs only. Your calls written first.
It makes each call and names the rule it used. A mismatch is a rule you've never put into words. Add it, then re-test in a new chat: the chat you built it in already knows your answers.
Your past decisions are the check from outside the tool. It can't agree with an answer it hasn't seen.

AI projects don't fail more. They fail differently.

Treat the demo as the start of the hard part.

Deliver · The 30-day gate

Scale. Pivot. Or kill.

Judge the workflow against the criteria you set before you started.
Scale
It met the measure with a light review.
Hand it over for good. Keep the check its corner calls for.
Pivot
Close, but the check costs too much.
Change the brief, the step, or who leads. Run another month.
Kill
It missed, or the errors mattered.
Keep it human. Revisit next quarter; the tools will have moved.
Killing is a good outcome. You found where the line is, cheaply.

Revisit · Every few months

The corners move. The check stays.

If you lead people

Your juniors lost the easy reps.

Say the reasoning out loud.
When you fix their work, explain why the standard is the standard. They used to absorb that through repetition.
