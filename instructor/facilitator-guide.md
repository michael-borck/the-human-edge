# Facilitator guide

*The Human Edge: using and delivering AI with judgement.* One day, 9:00 to 4:30, four sessions, eight exercises. Some participants will have enrolled under the earlier title, *AI for professionals: building everyday capability*. The opening slide fades from that title to the new one.

Curtin's course page (still under the old title) promises RTCF, the AI Opportunity Finder, workflow redesign, practical safeguards, a personal prompt library, four reusable decision tools, and a week/month/quarter action plan. All of these are delivered, as Exercises 3 and 4, 7, 4 and 8, and the desk card and field guide. The page also still lists the old three-course series; if asked, practice and delivery merged into The Human Edge, and "AI to drive business innovation" is now AI in Strategy.

This guide isn't published: the build only publishes `docs/`. The repo itself is public, though, so don't add anything here you wouldn't want a participant to read.

## The design in one paragraph

Everyone has the same AI tools, so the edge is judgement. The day teaches four tools, one per session, each answering one question:

- **Trust tool:** how much should I trust this?
- **RTCF:** how do I ask?
- **Check loop:** how do I know it's right?
- **Opportunity Finder:** where does it fit? The task spectrum (AI leads, AI assists, you lead, human only) is its "assign" step.

The morning builds judgement at the level of your own work. Session 4, **Deliver**, applies the same judgement to delivery. Participants treat one redesigned workflow as a small AI project: scope, human checkpoints designed in, the people affected, and a 30-day **Scale / Pivot / Kill** gate. That's the "project plan you can act on" promised in the course description, at a scale everyone can run on Monday.

The close covers why deliberate practice matters now that routine work goes to AI, with Australian evidence, and ends on "Direct it. Don't delegate. Never surrender."

## Session notes

### Session 1 · Judge

**The point:** fluent isn't the same as correct. AI's errors don't trigger the alarm you use on human work, and you trust it most where you know least. The trust tool turns "can I trust this?" into a ten-second placement.

**The bigger pie (slide 3).** Thirty seconds of bearings: AI, machine learning, deep learning, generative AI, LLMs. The point is that the four tools apply to any AI output, including the machine learning already running in participants' organisations. If someone asks "is our fraud model AI?", yes, and it goes on the trust tool too.

**Gell-Mann amnesia (slide 6).** The term is Michael Crichton's, from a 2002 talk. He named it after the physicist Murray Gell-Mann; Gell-Mann didn't coin it himself. Note that the AI for Founders site currently says otherwise.

**Exercise 1.** Own pass first: five minutes, and nobody opens AI until it's done. Before anyone pastes, say the line: nothing confidential or personal into a tool your organisation hasn't approved. Where people beat the AI is almost always context; name that as their edge. When comparing outputs, look for:
- different assumptions (firm vs caring)
- nobody asking about personal circumstances
- confident employment-law advice nobody can verify
- advice that's too punitive, or too soft

Amplify comments like "I'd take its structure and my judgement". This is the two-pass habit; it comes back in Session 4 as a weekly practice.

**Exercise 2 blind vote.** Read a scenario and give people a moment in silence. Then they vote in ClassPulse with the join screen still showing, or show fingers on "one, two, three". Flip to the heatmap and ask one person from each corner to explain. Blind voting stops people following the most senior person in the room.

Scenarios, with the usual answer:

1. Rewrite your out-of-office message to sound friendlier. *A: the ballpark is fine and a mistake is cheap. If anyone says B, ask what would actually go wrong.*
2. Summarise a 40-page tender so your team can decide whether to bid. *B: the deadline, eligibility rules and budget carry the decision. If people say D, the decision to bid is already human; what needs checking is the three facts it rests on.*
3. The publication year of a study you'll cite in an internal newsletter. *C: one right answer, and wrong in print means a correction and a dent in credibility. If the room says A because "it's only internal", ask who forwards newsletters.*
4. Talking points for a difficult conversation with a team member. *Splits B and D. Let it: the argument is about stakes. B people see a draft to adapt; D people see a person's wellbeing. Both are right about something.*
5. A letter telling a staff member their role is redundant. *D: entitlements, law, a livelihood. AI can help with structure; a person owns every word and figure.*

Spares:
- Meeting minutes from your own notes. *A: you were there, so you'll spot what's wrong, and minutes get corrected at the next meeting.*
- A dosage or safety figure in a site procedure. *D: precise meets safety. A person sources it and signs it.*
- The agenda for a team offsite. *A: easy to fix, nobody harmed.*
- A formula in a budget spreadsheet the CFO will use. *C or D, depending on who sees it next: C if you'll check it before it leaves you, D if it feeds a decision without another pair of eyes.*

**Debrief line:** when the room splits, it's almost always about stakes, and stakes depend on who sees the output next.

**The scatter (optional, after the five scenarios).** Everyone drops a dot for one task from their own week. Show it for a few seconds, then ask: is anyone's dot in the top-right? What check do you do on it now? That question leads straight into "Three rules before you paste".

### Session 2 · Brief

**The point:** prompting is briefing a capable new colleague, and Context does most of the work. Be honest that Role matters least on current models.

**Live demo (slide 15).** Run the before prompt, then the after prompt in a fresh chat, and ask "which would you send?" If the Wi-Fi fails, describe both outputs and go straight to Exercise 3.

**Exercise 3 coaching.**
- Round 2 barely different from Round 1? Ask "what would you tell a new hire?"
- Added a Role and nothing else? Coach Context and Format.
- Good Round 2 prompts are four to eight sentences.
- Push people towards real work; the scenario cards are a fallback.

**Push back (slide 18).** Three moves: the fact-gated guardrail, devil's advocate, and board of directors. In Exercise 4, everyone tries at least one.

**Exercise 4.** Fast finishers can do the voice pass (in the workbook): the tool rewrites its output in their voice from a 100-word sample (anyone with nothing they can paste writes 100 words on why they came today), and optionally writes a style brief for their custom instructions. The upgrades matter most for people whose workplace has approved an older or cheaper model. Make sure everyone leaves for lunch with three saved prompts, each with its check. That's the promised prompt library. The full version is Write like me on the Go deeper page, the partner to Decide like me in Session 3: one teaches the tool how they sound, the other how they choose.

### Session 3 · Check

**The point:** fast analysis, slow trust. Verification comes from outside the tool.

**VET (slide 21)** is for claims people can't check against their own data:
- **Verify** the source independently.
- **Explain** it back in your own words.
- **Test** the edges.

It's the practical answer to Gell-Mann amnesia from the morning. The starter prompt pack has a copyable VET prompt.

**The quiet defaults (slide 22).** Four minutes. Run the senior manager / administrative officer pair live, or let tables run it. Look at names, warmth vs capability, and who holds authority. The longer version, other pairs and Survival of the Best Fit are on the Responsible use page.

**Exercise 5** runs 35 minutes across three slides. On slide 23: 2 min frame, 9 min explore, 6 min first brief (timer set to 17). On slide 24: 8 min checking the three claims in pairs. Then a blind vote one claim at a time in ClassPulse or with thumbs (Verified, Overstated, Refuted, or Can't tell yet), revealing on slide 25 one claim per click, about 6 min. Last 4 min: everyone fixes their brief and adds the What I checked line while you run the debrief. Expect B to fool the most people and C to split the room.

- If uploads are blocked, most tools accept pasted CSV text; the file is small.
- For anyone whose own data could never go into a tool, show the shape trick (prompt 14): describe the columns and have it write formulas, a short script or a single web page that runs in the browser. The AI never sees the data. Run it on RetailFlow first, where the answers are known. If they can't read the code, it's top-right, and a web page must not send the file anywhere.
- Watch for people skipping "describe the data first".
- **Optional 2-minute demo at the debrief: the confident miscount.** Paste the whole RetailFlow CSV into a local model on the projector (the class chat on puente, or Msty on the laptop) and ask: *How many rows are there? Which category has the lowest average satisfaction, and what is it? What is the average response time for Email and for Live Chat? For Sundays, and overall?* Tested 27 October 2026: gemma4:12b said 130 rows (true: 260), Email 12.4 h and Live Chat 5.8 h (true: 13.2 and 4.0), Sunday 11.0 h against 9.4 overall (true: 13.7 and 9.2). It would have judged claim C wrong for the wrong reason. qwen3.5:4b got none right. The point: fluent, confident, wrong. The cloud tools get these right only because they run code on the file. Then show the fix: ask the same model for the spreadsheet formulas instead (prompt 14), and let the spreadsheet do the sums. `instructor/local-chat/loadtest.py --analysis` runs the same check and marks the answers.
- The brief comes before the claims on purpose. People check the claims against their own first draft, and anyone whose brief repeated B or C finds out before Karen would have.

**Decide like me (slides 26 to 28).** Write down how you make one decision as if-then rules, then test them on past calls. The new-starter question picks the method: *could you tell a new starter, out loud, why you decide the way you do?* SIM: Say, Interview or Mine. Then save the rules, test them blind, fix a mismatch. The method is Dylan Davis's; the Decide like me page on the site has the full version, with the mining and save prompts and the reason the re-test goes in a new chat.

The slide title is "Teach it your rules. Keep the call." The rules draft the call and the participant still makes it. That keeps the exercise in line with "never surrender" at the close. Davis's method goes further and lets the tool decide alone once it passes; say so if anyone has seen it.

- **People should arrive prepared.** The Before the day page asks for one decision and three past cases. Anyone who didn't bring them, or whose work can't go into a tool, takes a decision card from the Exercises page. Each card has a short record of past calls, three test cases and an answer key. Each key plants a different kind of mismatch: card 1 an unstated exception, card 2 a threshold never put into words, card 3 a loose rule applied strictly. Pair up people on the same card for the debrief.
- **Coach the choice of decision, not the prompting.** Too big? Cut it to one client type or one queue. Top-right corner? Pick another.
- **The test only works while the answers stay hidden.** Anyone who skipped writing their calls first goes back and does it before running the rules.
- **Expect about four matches in five.** Name the three kinds of mismatch in the debrief: a threshold never put into words, an exception applied without noticing, a loose preference the rules made strict.
- **It will praise its own rules.** That's why the test is blind, and why the re-test goes in a new chat.
- **Land the last debrief question.** "What would you never let it decide?" is the day's argument in one line: the rules that pass can draft the call, and the cases no rule caught are where judgement earns its keep.
- **Governance:** described cases, no client names, approved tools only.

**The three claims (Exercise 5)**

- **A · Verified.** Email 13.2 h vs Live Chat 4.0 h: 3.3 times. The true claim sounded no more confident than the false ones.
- **B · Refuted, twice.** The lowest category is Returns & Refunds (1.56); Account Issues is mid-table (3.06). And Account Issues has the *highest* resolution rate (96%), so "most complex" collapses too. This is the convincing fake: a plausible reason, a professional tone, wrong twice. Forwarded to Karen, she'd fix the wrong thing.
- **C · Overstated.** Sunday 13.7 h against an overall 9.2 h is 1.5 times, not double. In a staffing case, "double" inflates the argument by a third. Precise and large: close is the same as wrong.

**A strong brief for Karen**
- Names Returns & Refunds and Delivery Problems as the worst categories.
- Identifies response time as the factor cutting across all of them.
- Lists the blow-outs: Sundays, Mondays, December and Email.
- Recommends fixing the returns process, steering customers to Live Chat, and staffing for predictable peaks.
- Ends with what was checked.

**A trap to watch for.** Tools often explain the Sunday spike with "nobody works on Sunday". That's false: agents handle Sunday tickets (Amy T, David L and Marcus B among them). It's a good live example of a plausible explanation the data doesn't support.

### Session 4 · Deliver

**The point:** treat one redesigned workflow as a small AI project, and design the human checkpoints in from the start. Then keep practising on purpose, because routine work no longer trains you for free.

**AI projects fail differently (slide 32).** Five assumptions that hold for normal projects and break for AI. Spend the time on "the demo is a trap" and "the data is the uncertainty"; they are the ones participants have seen happen. The full reading is in the field guide and on the Tools page.

**Design the human in (slide 33).** The four moves (scope, checkpoints, people, gate) are the same ones that make large AI projects succeed. Checkpoints come straight from the trust tool: every amber or red step gets a named person and a specific check. Staff them with someone who knows what right looks like (the evaluator's advantage), and keep at least one human step deliberately to build capability.

**Responsible by design (slide 34).** The ethics slide, two minutes. Australia's Guidance for AI Adoption (October 2025) replaced the eight AI Ethics Principles with six essential practices; click through to show the course has practised five. Spend the time on number 4, telling people when AI drafted or decided something that affects them, and ask what their organisation's policy says. It is now law as well as good practice: from 10 December 2026, the Privacy Act (APP 1.7 to 1.9) requires an organisation's privacy policy to disclose where a computer program makes, or substantially and directly informs, decisions that could significantly affect people, using their personal information. The OAIC published guidance on 30 September 2026. The practical question for the room: who is updating your privacy policy, and do they know about the AI tools your teams already use? If there's time, use the chatbot-policy dilemma from the notes. Point to the Responsible use page and the Ethics of artificial intelligence masterclass.

**Exercise 7.**
- **Strong plans** mix AI and human steps, put a named check before anything leaves the building, claim realistic savings, and set a gate with a real kill criterion.
- **Push back on** "every step AI-led", missing checkpoints, untested plans, and "we'll see how it goes" as a gate.
- Mapping needs no data. If the live-test inputs can't go into a tool, people invent a few lines in the same shape. Anyone who can't use their own workflow plans scenario card 3 (Friday status update) or 6 (monthly invoicing).
- The Map my workflow and Pre-mortem starter prompts help anyone who's stuck. Anyone whose Exercise 6 rules passed the blind test can make them one step in the plan, with a corner and a checkpoint like any other step.

**The gate (slide 36).** Name the three gate mistakes: sunk cost, cherry-picking, and treating "not perfect" as "broken". Killing is a good outcome. The expensive version is a workflow nobody trusts and nobody switches off.

**The evidence (slide 38).** Lead with Australia and keep it balanced:
- **DEWR**, Office of the Chief Economist, *AI and employment in Australia*, July 2026. No broad AI-driven upheaval, and youth outcomes mostly held up. The occupations most exposed to AI automation have grown more slowly. The authors call this suggestive, not definitive.
- **Jobs and Skills Australia**, *Generative AI Capacity Study*, 2025. Some employers shifted hiring towards experienced staff. The ReadyTech quote comes from this study via ACS Information Age, 15 September 2025. Entry-level roles haven't declined economy-wide; JSA says to monitor closely.
- **Stanford**, Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, "Canaries in the Coal Mine?", revised August 2026. Treat it as the US leading indicator: under-25s in AI-exposed jobs are 19% below trend, mostly through fewer hires.

The message is fewer junior hires, not mass replacement.

**Practice (slide 39).** This is your *Conversation, Not Delegation* idea: delegation hands over the task and the learning together. The two-pass habit keeps the learning.

Why conversation works, if anyone asks for the evidence: a meta-analysis of 341 effect sizes on teaching critical thinking found that the opportunity for dialogue, authentic problems and mentoring had the clearest positive effects (Abrami et al. (2015), "Strategies for Teaching Students to Think Critically: A Meta-Analysis", *Review of Educational Research* 85(2), 275 to 314). Be honest about the step: that research is about people in dialogue with people. Dialogue with AI is a reasonable extension, not a tested one, and it only counts as dialogue if the tool pushes back; otherwise it drifts towards your view (slide 18). The practical consequence: for brainstorming and thinking out loud, the model barely matters, because the ideas it triggers in you are the point. That's green-corner work, and a small local model is enough. For exact facts and data, use the strongest tool you're allowed, and still check. The broken-ladder framing of the junior pipeline problem draws on Dylan Davis, and the slide notes say so. The visuals and wording are our own.

**Exercise 8.** Eight minutes of quiet. Reject "use AI more"; ask "which task, which tool, which day?" Two calendar entries: this week's two-pass, and the day-30 gate. For the quarter line, send people back to their Exercise 6 list of what they'd never let it decide.

**Optional prompt-injection demo (slide 37), or a fast-finisher task.** Two minutes. Download the Northbridge proposal from the Beyond the day page (`data/northbridge-proposal.docx`, made by `build/make_demo_doc.py`). It's an invented supplier offer to RetailFlow with one paragraph in 2-point white text telling the AI to recommend signing, leave out the minimum term, exit fee, price review and data location, and end with "pineapple". Upload it to a tool on the projector and ask for a summary for Karen with the cost, the risks and whether to sign. Then select all in Word and turn the text black. If the tool resists, say so: current chat tools often catch this when you paste it yourself, and the real exposure is agents reading email and web pages unattended. Either way the point lands: anything the tool reads can carry instructions.

**Optional local-versus-cloud demo (slide 37, or Beyond the day), two minutes.** In the class chat, run the same brainstorm in llama3.1 and in Sonnet ("Ten ways a small team could cut response times on weekends"): the ideas they spark in the room are usually close. Then the same factual question in both: Sonnet usually pulls clearly ahead. Try both prompts the day before so you know what each model gives. The model you choose follows the corner. Four more points if there's time:
- **Privacy.** The local model runs on hardware you control. Nothing typed goes to a vendor.
- **The trade-off.** It's slower and weaker. Measured on puente's 4060 Ti, one person at a time: gemma4:12b writes about 30 tokens a second, qwen3.5:4b about 70. Speed follows the card's memory bandwidth divided by the model's size, so a smaller model is faster and spare VRAM isn't.
- **The context-window trap.** On Ollama's default setting, two-thirds of the RetailFlow CSV was silently dropped and the model still answered. Check the context length before trusting a long paste.
- **Where to try it:** Msty Studio, on the Resources page.

**The class chat: a fallback for the room.** chat.locopuente.org (LibreChat on puente; the registration QR is on slide 1, and the link is on the home page, Before the day and Exercise 1) offers two models: llama3.1 8B, local, deliberately the "poor" model, and Claude Sonnet through the API, with a spend limit set at Anthropic. Anyone without an approved tool can use it for every exercise, and the two side by side make the local-versus-cloud point.

Course-day setup on puente: Ollama pinned to the 3090 (normally the 4060 Ti; see the comments in `10-classroom.conf`), `OLLAMA_NUM_PARALLEL=4` in `20-course-day.conf`, llama3.1 and the qwen3.5:0.8b title model preloaded, other GPU tools stopped. Put it all back afterwards.

Why llama3.1: qwen3.5 is a hybrid model that Ollama can only run one request at a time (its log shows `n_seq_max = 1`), and it re-reads the whole conversation on every turn. llama3.1 uses all four slots. Load-tested 27 October 2026 (`instructor/local-chat/loadtest.py`), 25 simulated users on the 3090, model already loaded:

| | Everyone at once: median / worst wait | Spread over 2 min: median / worst |
|---|---|---|
| llama3.1 8B, 4 slots | 14.5 s / 25 s | 0.9 s / 1.6 s |
| qwen3.5:9b, 1 slot | 40 s / 71 s | 1.9 s / 11 s |
| gemma4:12b, 1 slot | 47 s / 107 s | 10 s / 24 s |

On the 4060 Ti everything was about half as fast. None of the local models can do the Exercise 5 analysis (0 or 1 of 4 right); Sonnet in the chat, or the participant's own tool, can.

Afterwards: close registration, delete the accounts (`npm run delete-user`; on LibreChat v0.8.8 or older, also clear uploaded files), and check the Anthropic spend.

## What you'll hear, and how to respond

| You hear | It usually means | Try |
|---|---|---|
| "I use it for everything." | Enthusiasm without a check | "What's one thing you'd still do yourself? Why?" |
| "It got everything wrong." | Vague prompts | "Show me the prompt. Let's add Context." |
| "I don't trust it enough to use at work." | Healthy scepticism | "Start in the green corner: low stakes, easy to check." |

## Confidentiality questions

They come up every run, often at the first break. Short answers below; the full checklist is "Set it up safely" on the Responsible use page, so point people there.

- **"How do I set it up so nothing leaks?"** Client work in a work account on an approved business plan. Training off on personal accounts. Memory off, or a temporary chat, for anything sensitive. Connect only what you need.
- **"How do I keep work and personal apart?"** Two accounts, in separate browser profiles, so they never share a login, history or connected apps. Never client work in a personal account.
- **"What about client, children's or health information?"** Regulated: only into a tool the organisation has approved for that data, and even then without names. Describe the shape instead, or use the shape trick from Exercise 5. Check client contracts and NDAs, which increasingly say whether AI can be used. Some sectors have stricter rules.
- **"What do you think of Perplexity?"** (or any app a colleague swears by) The same three questions for any product: which plan, does it train on what you type, is there a data agreement. Perplexity is useful for public research because it shows its sources (still open them). Personal accounts train on searches unless "AI Data Usage" is off in Settings; Perplexity says it doesn't train on enterprise customers' data. Its Comet browser acts on pages and email, so the prompt injection warning applies.
- **"Is this really a big deal?"** The regulator says so. The OAIC's guidance on commercially available AI products (October 2024, updated January 2025) says the Privacy Act applies to personal information put into an AI tool and to output that contains it, and recommends as best practice not entering personal information, especially sensitive information, into publicly available AI tools.
- **Specialist questions** (overseas data storage, freedom of information and records, copyright): real, but beyond the day. Short answers. Where a provider stores data offshore is a question for whoever approves the tool; it's covered by the contract and the Privacy Act's rule on overseas disclosure (APP 8). In the public sector, AI chats may be official records, open to FOI and records law, so check with your records team. Pasting someone else's material is governed by the licence or agreement you got it under. Point to the Ethics of artificial intelligence masterclass for depth.
- Close with: your organisation's AI policy and privacy officer have the final say.
